26  Multiple Comparisons

Draft

This chapter is a draft, written with an AI assistant (Claude) on 29 October 2026, and not yet reviewed by the instructor. It may be substantially rewritten or removed.

A p-value below 0.05 is meant to be a rare event when there is no effect: it happens by chance only 5% of the time. But this holds for one test. When we run many tests, rare events become common: if we look at enough comparisons, some will be “significant” just by chance. This chapter explains the problem and the main corrections, without the mathematics behind them.

This chapter is original material written for this book; it is not part of Irizarry’s Introduction to Data Science.

26.1 The problem

Let’s simulate it. A study compares two groups of 20 participants on several outcome measures, and none of the measures truly differs between the groups. We run one t-test per measure and record whether at least one p-value is below 0.05. We repeat the whole study 1,000 times, for studies with 1, 5, 20 and 50 measures:

set.seed(1)
at_least_one <- function(k, n = 20) {
  p <- replicate(k, t.test(rnorm(n), rnorm(n))$p.value)
  any(p < 0.05)
}
k <- c(1, 5, 20, 50)
prop <- sapply(k, function(k) mean(replicate(1000, at_least_one(k))))
data.frame(tests = k, prop_at_least_one_significant = prop)
#>   tests prop_at_least_one_significant
#> 1     1                         0.057
#> 2     5                         0.228
#> 3    20                         0.625
#> 4    50                         0.926

With one test, about 5% of studies find a “significant” difference that does not exist, as expected. With 20 tests, most studies find at least one. Nothing is wrong with each single test: the problem is the number of chances we give ourselves.

26.2 What counts as a family of tests?

A correction is applied to a family of tests: the set of tests whose results are interpreted together. Common families in behavioural research are:

  • all the pairwise comparisons after an ANOVA (post-hoc tests, Chapter 25);
  • all the effects in the table of a multiway ANOVA: main effects and interactions. A three-factor ANOVA already has seven tests, and when none of them was predicted in advance, they form a family too1;
  • the same comparison on several outcome measures (accuracy, reaction time, confidence ratings, …);
  • the same analysis in several subgroups (men and women, age groups, …).

26.3 Two things a correction can control

Corrections differ in what they protect us from:

  • The family-wise error rate (FWER) is the probability of making at least one false positive in the whole family. Controlling it at 5% means that, in 95% of studies, not a single one of the significant results is a false positive. This is a strict guarantee, appropriate when every single claim matters, as in a confirmatory study.
  • The false discovery rate (FDR) is the expected proportion of false positives among the significant results. Controlling it at 5% means accepting that, on average, about 1 in 20 of our “discoveries” is false, in exchange for finding more of the real effects. This is a more lenient guarantee, appropriate when we test many hypotheses to find promising ones, as in an exploratory study.

26.4 The main corrections

In R, all these corrections are applied with p.adjust(), which takes a vector of p-values and returns adjusted p-values that you compare with the usual 0.05. Functions such as pairwise.t.test() accept the same method names in their p.adjust.method argument.

  • Bonferroni ("bonferroni"). Controls the FWER in the strong sense: whatever the number of true and false null hypotheses, and whatever the dependence between the tests. It is simple and safe, but very conservative: with many tests, it makes it hard to detect real effects, so it trades false positives for false negatives.
  • Holm ("holm"). The same strong FWER control as Bonferroni, but always at least as powerful. There is no reason to prefer Bonferroni to Holm, and Holm is the default in pairwise.t.test() and p.adjust().
  • Benjamini–Hochberg ("BH", also called "fdr"). Controls the FDR2. It is much more powerful than Bonferroni and Holm when there are many tests, which makes it a common choice for exploratory analyses with many comparisons. The price is that some of the significant results are expected to be false. It is guaranteed to work when the tests are independent or positively related to each other, which covers most practical cases.
  • Benjamini–Yekutieli ("BY"). Also controls the FDR, but under any kind of dependence between the tests3. It is safer than Benjamini–Hochberg when the tests may be related in complex ways, but more conservative, sometimes nearly as strict as Bonferroni.
Method Controls Pros Cons
Bonferroni FWER (strong) simple; valid under any dependence very conservative with many tests
Holm FWER (strong) same guarantee, more power than Bonferroni still conservative with many tests
Benjamini–Hochberg FDR much more power with many tests some discoveries are expected to be false; assumes independent or positively related tests
Benjamini–Yekutieli FDR valid under any dependence more conservative than Benjamini–Hochberg

Here are eight p-values from one study, adjusted with the four methods:

p <- c(0.001, 0.008, 0.012, 0.021, 0.035, 0.048, 0.19, 0.42)
data.frame(p,
           bonferroni = p.adjust(p, method = "bonferroni"),
           holm = p.adjust(p, method = "holm"),
           BH = p.adjust(p, method = "BH"),
           BY = p.adjust(p, method = "BY")) |>
  round(3)
#>       p bonferroni  holm    BH    BY
#> 1 0.001      0.008 0.008 0.008 0.022
#> 2 0.008      0.064 0.056 0.032 0.087
#> 3 0.012      0.096 0.072 0.032 0.087
#> 4 0.021      0.168 0.105 0.042 0.114
#> 5 0.035      0.280 0.140 0.056 0.152
#> 6 0.048      0.384 0.144 0.064 0.174
#> 7 0.190      1.000 0.380 0.217 0.590
#> 8 0.420      1.000 0.420 0.420 1.000

Six of the original p-values are below 0.05. After correction, Bonferroni, Holm and Benjamini–Yekutieli keep only the first, while Benjamini–Hochberg keeps four. Which one is right depends on the question: if a single false claim would be costly, control the FWER (Holm); if you are screening many effects to follow up later, controlling the FDR (Benjamini–Hochberg) is reasonable. Whatever you choose, choose it before looking at the results, and report it.

26.5 Planned contrasts

Corrections are needed when we look at many comparisons without having said in advance which ones matter. A better strategy, when theory allows it, is to decide before collecting the data which few comparisons answer the research question, write them down (ideally in a preregistration), and test only those. These planned contrasts keep the family small, so the tests keep their power. For example, in the attention experiment of Chapter 25, the hypothesis “any distractor increases errors” can be tested with one contrast (no distractor vs the average of the two distractor conditions) instead of three post-hoc tests.

Good open sources on planned contrasts:

  • D. J. Schad, S. Vasishth, S. Hohenstein, and R. Kliegl (2020), How to capitalize on a priori contrasts in linear (mixed) models: A tutorial, Journal of Memory and Language, 110, 104038, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jml.2019.104038; freely available on arXiv.
  • The emmeans vignette Comparisons and contrasts, which works directly with models fitted with afex.
  • D. Navarro, Learning Statistics with R, Chapter 16, sections 16.7 Different ways to specify contrasts and 16.9 The method of planned comparisons.
